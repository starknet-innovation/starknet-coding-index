Does documentation access make an LLM a better Starknet developer?

We gave models the same 13 smart-contract tasks, with and without the Cairo Coder documentation tool: GLM 5.2 across five reasoning-effort settings, plus the best open-weight coder from each of seven Chinese labs at max thinking. One law explains all of it — the tool's value tracks the model's Cairo knowledge gap: zero for frontier models that saturate the suite, decisive for models that don't.

Starknet Coding Index (baseline, no assistance)

One number per model for "how good is this LLM at writing Starknet smart contracts today". Each model runs the full task suite alone (no documentation tool) with a compile-and-repair budget of 10 turns and 15 minutes of model time — a run that needs more than 15 minutes of thinking is a failure, however its tests end up. Where several thinking variants were benchmarked, the chart shows the model's best-scoring variant, with the thinking level labeled under each bar (e.g. low, xhigh; models with one fixed mode show that mode — Kimi K3 always runs at max). 39–130 runs per entry. The score blends five measurements:

  • Correctness (50%) — average fraction of hidden tests passed per task. Half the index: a fast, cheap model that writes wrong contracts cannot rank well.
  • One-shot rate (15%) — share of runs solved on the very first submission, no compiler feedback needed.
  • Speed (15%) and cost (15%) — median model latency (time spent waiting on the model's API, excluding this harness's local compile/test) and median $ per task, scored 100→0 on fixed log scales (10s→1200s, $0.003→$0.6).
  • Token efficiency (5%) — median output tokens per task (1k→40k), penalizing verbosity independent of price.

The scales are fixed, not relative — adding a new model later never changes an existing score.

MiMo-V2.5-Pro leads the composite by a hair: perfect correctness at near-best speed and cost outweighs Kimi K3's unmatched 90% one-shot rate. Sonnet 5, the first closed-weight entrant, sits within a point of the lead — it matches MiMo's perfect correctness and is the fastest model in the field (~14s median), but its API pricing (~$0.024 vs ~$0.004 median per task) caps its cost score and keeps it just behind. Notably, the best variant is often the cheapest one — GLM 5.2 and Hy3 score highest at @low, and Sonnet 5 and DeepSeek V4-Pro score highest with thinking off (DeepSeek holds ~97% correctness without reasoning at all, at 2–4× less time and money — worth +7.6 points and three places). Gemini 3.6 Flash, the second closed-weight entrant, debuts at #4 (best variant low): perfect correctness and the fastest per-task latency after Sonnet, but it thinks in volume — 2–5× MiMo's output tokens per task — so flash pricing still lands it at ~6× MiMo's cost per task. The GPT-5.6 pair breaks the closed-model pattern: OpenAI's Luna (#7, xhigh) and Terra (#6, off) are the first closed entrants below the saturation club — neither ever one-shots, both ship pro modes that cost 2–3× their max tiers for less correctness, and Terra's 2.5× price premium over Luna buys just +0.6 index points. On Cairo, OpenAI's scale ladder is a knowledge ladder with flat habits.

Where the tool matters — task difficulty

baselinewith MCP

Easy tasks don't need help. Mid-difficulty contracts — token variants, escrow, voting — are where documentation pays: failures disappear entirely (+14pt). The hardest tasks (account abstraction, components) improve but still fail sometimes: knowing the docs isn't the same as getting the architecture right.

Documentation substitutes for thinking — the effort curve

The less the model is allowed to think, the more the tool helps — and the more the model reaches for it (documentation lookups per run: disabled: 1.8low: 0.9medium: 0.8high: 0.7xhigh: 1.2). Extra thinking buys no solve-rate gain: after enlarging low/medium/high baselines to n=130, low and medium are statistically identical (p=0.83) and high trails by a suggestive-but-not-significant ~7pt (p=0.09) at roughly the same cost — while the xhigh tier spends ~2.4× the time and ~1.9× the money of low for a statistically indistinguishable solve rate.

For both leaders the thinking knob buys nothing on this suite — including switching it off. Sonnet 5 solves 195/195 at identical cost (~$0.024) and output (~1,700 tokens) at every tier because its thinking is adaptive: granted any budget, it declines to spend reasoning tokens on tasks it already knows. MiMo-V2.5-Pro solves 273/273 across all seven tiers with equally flat cost (~$0.005) and output (~1,500 tokens) — knowledge saturation again, this time in an open-weight model with a hard off switch. MiMo's apparent latency spread (19s at xhigh vs ~45s at the tiers benchmarked in a later batch) is a provider artifact, not an effort effect: the earlier batch was served at ~81 tok/s, the later at ~33 tok/s, and within a batch the tiers are flat. One-shot rates wobble without a monotone trend (MiMo 18–41%, Sonnet 46–67%) — binomial noise at n=39. Gemini 3.6 Flash is the third pattern: an obedient thinker. It spends whatever budget it is granted (2k→7.7k output tokens, $0.023→$0.061 per task, minimal→max) and the spend does buy something — one-shot rate climbs steadily from 10% to 46% — but not solves: it is at 100% from low upward regardless. GPT-5.6 Luna is the fourth pattern — and the first closed model that is NOT Cairo-saturated: its solve rate genuinely climbs with the dial (79.5% with thinking off → 100% at max), it never one-shots at any tier (median 3–4 compiler round-trips), and its pro serving mode burns 14.5k output tokens and ~$0.11 per task to score below max. Terra, Luna's 2.5×-price sibling, shows what that money buys: knowledge, not habits. Its thinking-off correctness jumps to 89.7% (Luna: 79.5%) and its curve flattens to noise (only medium reaches 100%), but it still never one-shots and its pro mode is again strictly dominated ($0.21/task for less than max). Net effect on the index: +0.6 points over Luna. Completing the open-model ladders sharpened the split. DeepSeek V4-Pro turns out not to need its thinking at all: ~95–97% at every tier including off, where it runs 2–4× faster and cheaper — the off tier jumps it to #5 on the index. Hy3's ladder is erratic: thinking off collapses it (87%→67%, the GLM pattern), but the dial isn't even monotone — minimal reasons longer than medium (303s vs 242s median) and both drown in over-budget grinds; low stays its sweet spot. MiniMax M3 genuinely needs its thinking (87%→74% descending the ladder) and its off switch is fake — the API accepts disabled and reasons anyway, so that pseudo-variant is excluded. The practical rule survives every pattern: run the cheapest tier that holds correctness; the thinking dial buys solve rate only where knowledge runs out (GLM, Hy3, MiniMax, Qwen — and Luna).

The knowledge-gap law holds across seven labs: MCP lift tracks baseline weakness — zero for the saturating frontier (Kimi K3, MiMo), +5pt in the 95% tier (DeepSeek, GLM), +8pt for Hy3, +18pt for the weakest entrant (Qwen3.6-27B, 23%→41%). Findings

effectivenessBaseline failures are training-data lag, and the tool fixes exactly that

Failed baseline runs get stuck on current Cairo idioms — most often the storage API, writing pre-2024 Map.read(key) instead of today's Map.entry(key).read() — and burn the whole 10-turn budget against the compiler. In the MCP condition one documentation lookup resolves it. This is the precise gap a RAG tool over current docs should close, and it does.

economicsThe tool pays for itself by preventing failures, not by speeding up wins

Each lookup takes ~15–30s, so on tasks the model can already do the MCP condition is sometimes slower. But a failed run costs ~10 turns of reasoning tokens — several times the lookup overhead. Net across the study: lower median cost at 4 of 5 effort settings.

behaviorTool use is selective and rises when reasoning is scarce

The model consults the docs roughly once every other run at high effort, and ~1.8 times per run with thinking disabled — typically after the first compiler error, not up front. Availability doesn't degrade into dependence.

caveatCairo Coder confabulates outside its index

Asked about a token standard we invented ("STRK77"), the service returned a complete, confident, fabricated Cairo interface. Within its indexed corpus it's accurate; agents consuming it get no signal when a query falls outside coverage. Worth fixing upstream.

Methodology

  • Harness: agentic repair loop, max 10 assistant turns. The model submits src/lib.cairo via a submit tool; the harness runs scarb build + snforge test against hidden tests and returns the output. Conditions are identical except the MCP condition also exposes assist_with_cairo, replicated exactly from @kasarlabs/cairo-coder-mcp v0.2.5.
  • Tasks: 13 hand-written Starknet contracts (4 easy / 5 medium / 4 hard incl. a SNIP-6 account and a custom component); every reference solution passes 100% of its tests, every stub fails.
  • Model: z-ai/glm-5.2 via OpenRouter, throughput-sorted routing, provider-default temperature; efforts via the unified reasoning parameter (disabled = enabled:false). Costs are OpenRouter-reported.
  • Solved = every hidden test passes within the budget: 10 turns and 15 minutes of model time (LLM + doc-tool wait; wall time is not used — it depends on harness concurrency). 2–3 reps per cell: a third rep breaks 1–1 ties (entries benchmarked before 2026-07-24 used a fixed 3).

Caveats

  • Model dependence, demonstrated: the GLM sections characterize GLM 5.2 (884 runs); the roster (78 runs per model) spans full saturation (K3, MiMo) to collapse (Qwen3.6-27B). Roster runs round-trip reasoning history and stream responses; GLM runs predate those harness fixes, which GLM's own data shows it did not need.
  • Five roster cells abandoned: repeated host-sleep/network stalls made 5 qwen/minimax baseline cells (of 390) unrecoverable within budget; they are counted as failures, consistent with their completed sibling reps (which failed in 10-turn slogs). Time/cost medians exclude them.
  • MCP backend, tested: @high's first 3 reps used the hosted api.cairo-coder.com; everything else used a self-hosted replica (same corpus re-ingested, same embedding/generation models). A direct A/B (39 runs each, identical tasks/effort) found identical effectiveness — 38/39 solved on both, same turn counts — so hosted-index staleness did not skew results; only lookup speed differs (~5× faster locally). Data is pooled.
  • Statistics: confirmation batches raised low/medium/high baseline cells to n=130 (others n=39). The apparent "low beats high" ordering at 3 reps did not survive: low ≈ medium (p=0.83), high trails non-significantly (p=0.09). Solve-rate claims here carry Wilson 95% CIs of roughly ±5pt at n=130 and ±9pt at n=39.
  • Hosted sunset: api.cairo-coder.com shuts down 2026-07-31; the replica replaces it for reruns.
