The Starknet Coding Index — and what documentation access adds

Fifteen models — the best open-weight coders from seven labs and the current closed models from Anthropic, Google, and OpenAI — each ran the same 13 hidden-test smart-contract tasks at every useful thinking setting, with and without the Cairo Coder documentation tool. Two headlines: Opus 4.8 leads the index at 90.5, the first model to one-shot the whole suite; and the tool's value tracks the model's Cairo knowledge gap — from +12 index points for the weakest entrant to nothing at the saturated top.

Starknet Coding Index (baseline, no assistance)

One number per model for "how good is this LLM at writing Starknet smart contracts today". Each model runs the full task suite alone (no documentation tool) with a compile-and-repair budget of 10 turns and 15 minutes of model time — a run that needs more than 15 minutes of thinking is a failure, however its tests end up.

Where several thinking variants were benchmarked, the chart shows the model's best-scoring variant, labeled in parentheses (models with one fixed mode show that mode — Kimi K3 always runs at max). 26–130 runs per entry. The score blends five measurements:

  • Correctness (50%) — average fraction of hidden tests passed per task. Half the index: a fast, cheap model that writes wrong contracts cannot rank well.
  • One-shot rate (15%) — share of runs solved on the very first submission, no compiler feedback needed.
  • Speed (15%) and cost (15%) — median model latency (time spent waiting on the model's API, excluding this harness's local compile/test) and median $ per task, scored 100→0 on fixed log scales (10s→1200s, $0.003→$0.6).
  • Token efficiency (5%) — median output tokens per task (1k→40k), penalizing verbosity independent of price.

The scales are fixed, not relative — adding a new model later never changes an existing score.

Grok 4.5 leads at 90.8 — a statistical tie with Opus 4.8 (90.5) decided by economics: identical perfect correctness, but under a cent per task against Opus's ~$0.045 (cost score 78 vs 49). Opus keeps the record that money can't buy: the only model to solve every task on the first submission, every time (100% one-shot at high; the previous record was Kimi K3's 90%). Fable 5 (#2) ties it on every component except the bill: same correctness, speed, and token profile, but at 2× the price its cost score decides the race — Anthropic's two flagships are separated by pricing alone. MiMo-V2.5-Pro (#3) remains the open-weight champion, ~20× cheaper per task than the leaders, and Sonnet 5 (#4) matches everyone's correctness at the field's best latency.

The best variant is usually the cheapest one: GLM 5.2 and Hy3 peak at @low; Sonnet 5 and DeepSeek V4-Pro peak with thinking off — DeepSeek holds ~97% correctness without reasoning at all, at 2–4× less time and money.

OpenAI's ladder — Luna (#10), Terra (#9), Sol (#7) — is a knowledge ladder with sticky habits. Off-tier correctness climbs 79.5% → 89.7% → 100% with scale, but the iterate-against-the-compiler habit barely bends: the smaller two never one-shot and Sol manages only 12–23%, so perfect correctness at flagship pricing still can't crack the top five. The family's pro serving modes remain strictly dominated. Google's Gemini 3.6 Flash (#6) pairs perfect correctness with near-best latency but thinks in volume — 2–5× MiMo's output tokens per task.

Anthropic's ladder tells the opposite story. Haiku 4.5 (#12) keeps the family trait — thinking off is its best mode — but sheds 20 correctness points against Sonnet and adds a failure mode its siblings lack: given a big thinking budget it overthinks its way from 89% down to 66%.

Gains: Qwen3.6-27B +23.1, Hy3 +10.1, GLM 5.2 +6.4, MiniMax M3 +5.1, Haiku 4.5 +4.3, GPT-5.6 Terra +1.3, Gemini 3.6 Flash +1.1, GPT-5.6 Sol +0.6. Two nuances the closed models added: a gain doesn't require missing knowledge (Gemini and Sol are 100% correct with or without the tool, but docs shorten their repair loops enough to win on efficiency), and the law applies per variant, not per model (Terra's best tier is its unsaturated off mode, where docs buy real solves).

Findings

lawThe tool's value tracks the knowledge gap — in any weight class

Documentation lift lines up with baseline weakness across all thirteen measured models: +12.1 for the weakest entrant (Qwen3.6-27B), +4.3 for Haiku 4.5 (the first closed-model gain), fading to zero at the saturated top (MiMo, K3, Opus-class models).

Two refinements from the closed models: the law applies per variant (Terra gains only at its unsaturated off tier), and saturated models can still gain a little when lookups shorten their repair loops (Gemini +1.1, Sol +0.6).

thinkingThe thinking dial rarely buys correctness — run the cheapest tier that holds it

Five patterns across fifteen models: adaptive thinkers that ignore the dial (Sonnet 5, Opus 4.8, Fable 5), an indifferent one (MiMo — 100% at all seven tiers), an obedient one that spends budget without needing it (Gemini), real curves where thinking buys solves (GLM, Luna, MiniMax), and one inverted curve (Haiku overthinks itself from 89% to 66%). In twelve of fifteen models the index-best variant is the cheapest tier that holds correctness.

habitsOne-shot ability is architectural — documentation can't buy it

OpenAI's entire ladder iterates against the compiler at every scale and price (0–23% one-shot from Luna to Sol), while Anthropic's flagships one-shot nearly everything (96–100%). The tool never changed a model's one-shot rate: GPT-5.6 Luna knows Cairo (97% correct) yet measured −1.4 with docs — a habit is not a knowledge gap.

Tool discipline is a habit too, and the costliest one to lack: offered the same docs, Anthropic's flagships never called them once (−1.2 to −1.9, pure schema overhead) while Grok 4.5 dutifully consulted them about once per run it didn't need — worth −13.1, the largest penalty in the study.

economicsPro-style serving modes are strictly dominated

Both measured pro modes (luna-pro, terra-pro) cost 2–3× their model's max tier and scored below it. Neither ever produced the best configuration of its model; sol-pro was not funded on that record.

effectivenessIn the GLM study, baseline failures are training-data lag — and the tool fixes exactly that

GLM 5.2's failed baseline runs get stuck on current Cairo idioms — most often the storage API, writing pre-2024 Map.read(key) instead of today's Map.entry(key).read() — and burn the whole 10-turn budget against the compiler. One documentation lookup resolves it; net effect was lower median cost at 4 of 5 effort settings, with lookups rising as thinking budget fell (~0.5/run at high effort, ~1.8/run with thinking off).

caveatCairo Coder confabulates outside its index

Asked about a token standard we invented ("STRK77"), the service returned a complete, confident, fabricated Cairo interface. Within its indexed corpus it's accurate; agents consuming it get no signal when a query falls outside coverage. Worth fixing upstream.

For both leaders the thinking knob buys nothing on this suite — including switching it off. Sonnet 5 solves 195/195 at identical cost (~$0.024) and output (~1,700 tokens) at every tier because its thinking is adaptive: granted any budget, it declines to spend reasoning tokens on tasks it already knows.

MiMo-V2.5-Pro solves 273/273 across all seven tiers with equally flat cost (~$0.005) and output (~1,500 tokens) — knowledge saturation again, this time in an open-weight model with a hard off switch. MiMo's apparent latency spread (19s at xhigh vs ~45s at the tiers benchmarked in a later batch) is a provider artifact, not an effort effect: the earlier batch was served at ~81 tok/s, the later at ~33 tok/s, and within a batch the tiers are flat. One-shot rates wobble without a monotone trend (MiMo 18–41%, Sonnet 46–67%) — binomial noise at n=39. Gemini 3.6 Flash is the third pattern: an obedient thinker. It spends whatever budget it is granted (2k→7.7k output tokens, $0.023→$0.061 per task, minimal→max) and the spend does buy something — one-shot rate climbs steadily from 10% to 46% — but not solves: it is at 100% from low upward regardless.

GPT-5.6 Luna is the fourth pattern — and the first closed model that is NOT Cairo-saturated: its solve rate genuinely climbs with the dial (79.5% with thinking off → 100% at max), it never one-shots at any tier (median 3–4 compiler round-trips), and its pro serving mode burns 14.5k output tokens and ~$0.11 per task to score below max.

Terra, Luna's 2.5×-price sibling, shows what that money buys: knowledge, not habits. Its thinking-off correctness jumps to 89.7% (Luna: 79.5%) and its curve flattens to noise (only medium reaches 100%), but it still never one-shots and its pro mode is again strictly dominated ($0.21/task for less than max). Net effect on the index: +0.6 points over Luna.

Completing the open-model ladders sharpened the split. DeepSeek V4-Pro turns out not to need its thinking at all: ~95–97% at every tier including off, where it runs 2–4× faster and cheaper — the off tier jumps it to #5 on the index. Hy3's ladder is erratic: thinking off collapses it (87%→67%, the GLM pattern), but the dial isn't even monotone — minimal reasons longer than medium (303s vs 242s median) and both drown in over-budget grinds; low stays its sweet spot. MiniMax M3 genuinely needs its thinking (87%→74% descending the ladder) and its off switch is fake — the API accepts disabled and reasons anyway, so that pseudo-variant is excluded.

Haiku 4.5 adds a fifth pattern — the overthinker — and the dataset's first inverted curve: thinking off solves 88.9% at 29s/$0.05, while high collapses to 65.6% at 102s/$0.17, burning ~18k output tokens spiraling through full 10-turn slogs. Granting the small model a big budget makes it strictly worse on every axis; per the bracket protocol its ladder was not extended further upward.

Opus 4.8 completes the Anthropic ladder at the opposite extreme: all three bracket tiers are indistinguishable at 100% solve, ~15s, ~1,400 tokens — its adaptive thinking simply doesn't engage on tasks it has mastered — and at high it one-shots the entire suite, 26 for 26. One family, four fates for the same dial: the small model overthinks itself into failure, the mid model ignores the dial, the big models transcend it — Fable 5's dial is inert until max (minimal and high return byte-identical cost/token medians at the adaptive floor; max finally engages real thinking for +4pt one-shot at 3× the cost), and its 78-run bracket produced zero errors, zero tiebreaks, and zero refusals.

GPT-5.6 Sol completes the rival ladder: full correctness saturation at flagship scale (100% at every bracket tier), a one-shot habit that finally cracks but only to 12–23%, and an interior best tier (low) — the first bracket where neither edge won, so the ladder was not extended in either direction.

The practical rule survives every pattern: run the cheapest tier that holds correctness; the thinking dial buys solve rate only where knowledge runs out (GLM, Hy3, MiniMax, Qwen — and Luna).

Yes — and the 15-minute model-time budget settles the one apparent exception. "Extra thinking buys no solve-rate gain" holds for DeepSeek (94.9% at both tiers, but the low tier answers ~2.2× faster for ~25% less money) and trivially for MiMo (100% everywhere — knowledge-saturated, effort irrelevant). The knowledge-gap law holds across seven labs: MCP lift tracks baseline weakness — zero for the saturating frontier (Kimi K3, MiMo), +5pt in the 95% tier (DeepSeek, GLM), +8pt for Hy3, +18pt for the weakest entrant (Qwen3.6-27B, 23%→41%). Methodology

  • Harness: agentic repair loop, max 10 assistant turns. The model submits src/lib.cairo via a submit tool; the harness runs scarb build + snforge test against hidden tests and returns the output. Conditions are identical except the MCP condition also exposes assist_with_cairo, replicated exactly from @kasarlabs/cairo-coder-mcp v0.2.5.
  • Tasks: 13 hand-written Starknet contracts (4 easy / 5 medium / 4 hard incl. a SNIP-6 account and a custom component); every reference solution passes 100% of its tests, every stub fails.
  • Model: z-ai/glm-5.2 via OpenRouter, throughput-sorted routing, provider-default temperature; efforts via the unified reasoning parameter (disabled = enabled:false). Costs are OpenRouter-reported.
  • Solved = every hidden test passes within the budget: 10 turns and 15 minutes of model time (LLM + doc-tool wait; wall time is not used — it depends on harness concurrency). 2–3 reps per cell: a third rep breaks 1–1 ties (entries benchmarked before 2026-07-24 used a fixed 3).

Caveats

  • Unequal depth by design: GLM 5.2 carries the deepest dataset (~1,300 runs across five efforts and both conditions, from the original pilot study) — it anchors the substitution-law finding and the n=130 statistics; newer entrants carry 26–39 runs per variant. GLM runs predate the streaming and reasoning-round-trip harness fixes, which its own data shows it did not need.
  • Six cells abandoned, one batch truncated: host-sleep/network stalls made 5 qwen/minimax baseline cells unrecoverable, and 1 qwen@high cell was cut when its batch was stopped manually; all are counted as failures, consistent with their completed sibling reps. The stopped batch also skipped its tiebreaker pass, leaving 11 qwen high/max cells at 2 disagreeing reps (scored as the 2-rep mean). Time/cost medians exclude abandoned cells.
  • MCP backend, tested: @high's first 3 reps used the hosted api.cairo-coder.com; everything else used a self-hosted replica (same corpus re-ingested, same embedding/generation models). A direct A/B (39 runs each, identical tasks/effort) found identical effectiveness — 38/39 solved on both, same turn counts — so hosted-index staleness did not skew results; only lookup speed differs (~5× faster locally). Data is pooled.
  • Statistics: confirmation batches raised low/medium/high baseline cells to n=130 (others n=39). The apparent "low beats high" ordering at 3 reps did not survive: low ≈ medium (p=0.83), high trails non-significantly (p=0.09). Solve-rate claims here carry Wilson 95% CIs of roughly ±5pt at n=130 and ±9pt at n=39.
  • Hosted sunset: api.cairo-coder.com shuts down 2026-07-31; the replica replaces it for reruns.
